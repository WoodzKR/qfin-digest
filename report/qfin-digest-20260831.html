ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThe relevant empirical question about momentum in exchange-traded funds (ETFs) is not whether it works, but whether simple operational rules can extract value from it. We show that pure momentum over a broad ETF universe creates no value: replicated across 3,215 investable U.S. ETFs, it delivers Sharpe ratios between −0.17 and +0.22 with drawdowns of 69% to 82%, systematically below passive investing. Yet three layers of rules available to any investor fully reverse that conclusion. We evaluate the 2001–2026 period (1,289 weeks) across the investable universe, charging a 20-basis-point transaction cost per side at every stage of the analysis. On top of replicating classic cross-sectional momentum, we add technical entry signals, a multi-horizon momentum ranking with mandatory diversification across super-categories within an eight-position portfolio, and an expanded family of 144 dynamic stop-loss and take-profit exit rules, all under an asymmetric rebalancing regime of biweekly purchases and weekly exit monitoring. The complete strategy delivers a Sharpe ratio above 1.3 and a maximum drawdown of −7%, beating passive investing by close to a full Sharpe point; the alpha concentrates in tail-risk reduction and is impaired only during the high-implied-volatility regime. The central implication for researchers and practitioners is that the value comes not from the technical signal but from the operational management layer that surrounds it.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTWe report a structured negative result together with the narrow positive result that survives beside it. A fivefactor composite equity screen over the Nasdaq-100, whose weights were set heuristically, is decomposed into two disjoint parameter groups: composite weights, three of whose five factors derive from an undated fundamentals snapshot, and price-derived selection gates, which are point-in-time clean. We prove that the first group is not identified in our data: a factor constant in time contributes a time-invariant column to the design matrix and cannot be separated from the intercept by any pooled estimator. Non-negative least squares on these data place 92% of fitted weight on the volatility term and return unstable zero sets that do not coincide with the contaminated factors; we report these fits as uninformative rather than as confirmation, and out-of-sample R 2 reaches-0.314. We therefore decline to tune the weights, and confirm by direct search that no re-weighting is supportable: across 69 overlapping score dates (2020-08 to 2026-05, ≈ 23 effective independent windows), with Newey-West and Hansen-Hodrick standard errors and a Romano-Wolf stepdown correction replacing the naive paired t, no perturbation to any weight attains significance; the largest effect (removing the earnings-revision factor, t NW =-2.37) has a stepdown-adjusted p = 0.156. ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThis paper investigates whether unsupervised machine learning can identify economically meaningful regimes in the US equity market and whether such regimes provide genuine forecasting or investment value. Using a daily dataset of 3,823 observations spanning 18 October 2010 to 31 December 2025, sixteen standardized market, volatility, and macro-financial features are reduced via Principal Component Analysis (eight components explaining 92.1% of variance) and partitioned using K-Means clustering, validated against a Gaussian Mixture Model. The optimal solution, selected by silhouette score, identifies two statistically and economically distinct regimes, subsequently labelled Bull / Low-Risk (n = 3,201; 83.7% of observations) and Bear / High-Risk (n = 622; 16.3%). The regimes differ significantly across mean return, volatility, VIX level, momentum, and drawdown (Welch t-tests, all p < .001; Cohen's d up to 2.94), and exhibit strong persistence, with Bull-to-Bull and Bear-to-Bear transition probabilities of 96.5% and 81.8% respectively. A chronological, one-step-ahead out-of-sample forecasting exercise compares Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting classifiers against a naive persistence benchmark ("tomorrow's regime equals today's regime"). The persistence benchmark achieved the highest out-of-sample accuracy (93.55%), exceeding Random Forest (91.89%), Logistic Regression (89.80%), and Gradient Boosting (89.01%). This finding does not support the hypothesis that supervised machine learning provides superior next-day regime forecasting relative to a simple persistence rule. A regime-conditioned tactical investment strategy (fully invested in Bull, fully divested in Bear, signal lagged by one day, 10 basis point transaction costs) reduced maximum drawdown relative to buy-and-hold (-28.20% versus-33.92%) but underperformed on both compound annual growth rate (5.80% versus 12.68%) and Sharpe ratio (0.546 versus 0.780). Forward-return analysis indicates that returns following Bear/High-Risk observations were, on average, higher than returns following Bull/Low-Risk observations at 1-, 5-, and 20-day horizons, offering a plausible explanation for the strategy's return shortfall despite its lower drawdown. The study concludes that regime identification is statistically robust and economically interpretable, but that regime identification is not equivalent to reliable regime forecasting or to superior risk-adjusted investment performance. The paper's contribution lies in this explicit separation of unsupervised classification, supervised forecasting, and economic usefulness-three distinct questions that are frequently conflated in applied machine learning finance research. A supplementary robustness check (Section XI.J), which widens the feature set to twenty-nine variables by restoring the raw price, volume, and moving-average series the main specification deliberately excludes, produces a weaker four-regime solution and a severe train/test distribution shift that collapses out-of-sample forecasting performance-a negative result that, if anything, reinforces the original feature-exclusion rationale in Section IV.
