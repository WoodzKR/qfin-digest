ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThis paper investigates whether unsupervised machine learning can identify economically meaningful regimes in the US equity market and whether such regimes provide genuine forecasting or investment value. Using a daily dataset of 3,823 observations spanning 18 October 2010 to 31 December 2025, sixteen standardized market, volatility, and macro-financial features are reduced via Principal Component Analysis (eight components explaining 92.1% of variance) and partitioned using K-Means clustering, validated against a Gaussian Mixture Model. The optimal solution, selected by silhouette score, identifies two statistically and economically distinct regimes, subsequently labelled Bull / Low-Risk (n = 3,201; 83.7% of observations) and Bear / High-Risk (n = 622; 16.3%). The regimes differ significantly across mean return, volatility, VIX level, momentum, and drawdown (Welch t-tests, all p < .001; Cohen's d up to 2.94), and exhibit strong persistence, with Bull-to-Bull and Bear-to-Bear transition probabilities of 96.5% and 81.8% respectively. A chronological, one-step-ahead out-of-sample forecasting exercise compares Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting classifiers against a naive persistence benchmark ("tomorrow's regime equals today's regime"). The persistence benchmark achieved the highest out-of-sample accuracy (93.55%), exceeding Random Forest (91.89%), Logistic Regression (89.80%), and Gradient Boosting (89.01%). This finding does not support the hypothesis that supervised machine learning provides superior next-day regime forecasting relative to a simple persistence rule. A regime-conditioned tactical investment strategy (fully invested in Bull, fully divested in Bear, signal lagged by one day, 10 basis point transaction costs) reduced maximum drawdown relative to buy-and-hold (-28.20% versus-33.92%) but underperformed on both compound annual growth rate (5.80% versus 12.68%) and Sharpe ratio (0.546 versus 0.780). Forward-return analysis indicates that returns following Bear/High-Risk observations were, on average, higher than returns following Bull/Low-Risk observations at 1-, 5-, and 20-day horizons, offering a plausible explanation for the strategy's return shortfall despite its lower drawdown. The study concludes that regime identification is statistically robust and economically interpretable, but that regime identification is not equivalent to reliable regime forecasting or to superior risk-adjusted investment performance. The paper's contribution lies in this explicit separation of unsupervised classification, supervised forecasting, and economic usefulness-three distinct questions that are frequently conflated in applied machine learning finance research. A supplementary robustness check (Section XI.J), which widens the feature set to twenty-nine variables by restoring the raw price, volume, and moving-average series the main specification deliberately excludes, produces a weaker four-regime solution and a severe train/test distribution shift that collapses out-of-sample forecasting performance-a negative result that, if anything, reinforces the original feature-exclusion rationale in Section IV.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTLarge language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to discover trading strategies, and much of the resulting literature shares a methodological weakness: many candidate strategies are generated, the best is reported, and neither look-ahead bias nor the intensity of the search behind the reported result is corrected for. We present a strategy-discovery system that makes both corrections structural rather than procedural. First, the agent can only act through registry-validated tools whose feature space excludes look-ahead by construction; we show that this guardrail is not redundant with statistical correction: a deliberately leaky oracle posting a Sharpe ratio of 35 survives Deflated Sharpe and probability-of-backtest-overfitting testing completely. Second, the system records every strategy evaluation its search performs and deflates all reported performance by that trial count, tracing how the best in-sample Sharpe ratio climbs with each trial while the deflation threshold, driven by the agent's own search, climbs faster. Across a 453-stock point-in-time US equity universe and a 39-ETF multi-asset universe with realistic transaction, impact, and borrow costs, honest evaluation certifies passive benchmarks (out-of-sample confidence intervals excluding zero), rejects every LLM-discovered strategy (across two frontier models, search budgets up to one hundred candidates, and five repeated runs), catching selection luck, predicted rank degradation, and out-of-sample collapse through complementary instruments, and evaluates a human trader's production rule system under identical instruments. The framework formalizes why pre-registered hypotheses earn lower evidential bars than brute search, and quantifies the sample sizes that credible certification of moderate edges actually requires.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTIn financial markets, a sequential policy that reacts systematically to price movements may become predictable to other market participants. This paper studies whether large language model (LLM) agents exhibit such directional structure through RetailAgent, an experimental framework in which an LLM observes anonymized intraday equity price histories and permitted state, then repeatedly chooses long (hold the stock) or flat (stay out) before the subsequent interval return is revealed. We compare returns during long and flat intervals along the same stock's intraday path after removing the overall fraction of long decisions. This exposure-matched measure reveals persistent negative timing across modality, horizon, state, and model family. Shuffling saved action sequences substantially attenuates the effect, showing that alignment between actions and subsequent returns drives the negative score. Feeding self-authored memories into decisions further increases policy persistence, while timing becomes more negative among stock-days on which the agent uses both actions. These results reveal stable, recoverable directional structure in sequential LLM financial decisions and a behavioral signal for studying how another participant could respond to a predictable policy.
