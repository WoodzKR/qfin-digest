Another Moment for CAPM

코스큐니스를 쪼개니 분산-분산 상관만 값이 매겨졌다

Splitting coskewness shows only its variance-variance part is priced.

  • 비가우시안 CAPM의 3차 위험은 실증에서 늘 약했다

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThe non-Gaussian CAPM states that beta and coskewness are the only two systematic risks: beta captures second-order risk, while coskewness captures third-order risk. However, the empirical performance of coskewness is known to be weak. Using a return-moment expansion, I decompose coskewness into a sum of correlations between stock and market moments. If investors care less about higher-order risks, these components should receive unequal prices. In Fama-MacBeth regressions of individual stocks excluding microcaps, only the lowest-order component, the variance-variance correlation, earns a significant premium; higher-order cross-moment correlations do not. Thus, third-order risk is priced, but in a more nuanced way. A strategy combining beta and the variance-variance correlation earns about 9% annually, unexplained by Fama-French factors.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTFactor models conventionally form portfolios annually using stale financial statements released months earlier. ORIGINAL ABSTRACTKelly, Malamud and Zhou (2024, KMZ) show that highly complex prediction models can outperform simple ones when forecasting U.S. stock returns. ORIGINAL ABSTRACTMany cross-sectional factors, such as industry-relative ratios, abnormal accruals, and the residual of a same-period regression, require the full cross-section of peer firms. Because firms file on staggered dates, a characteristic constructed shortly after a fiscal period closes is estimated from only those firms that have already reported. We term the resulting concern incomplete-cross-section leakage and assess whether it biases backtested performance. The conventional diagnostic, namely the disagreement between the early-filing signal and the complete-cross-section signal, does not identify the bias, because that disagreement combines three components: sampling variation from the smaller subsample, a difference in the covariate composition of the early filers, and any dependence of filing speed on the component of the signal under study. Only the third constitutes leakage. We isolate it by holding the covariate composition fixed, either through a matched control group or, equivalently, through the partial correlation between filing latency and the regression residual conditional on the regressors. This partial correlation is the selection-bias term of a Heckman model; because it is considerably less noisy than the disagreement statistic, we adopt it as the primary measure and provide it as an ex-ante screen, validated on a controlled experiment with a known planted bias. We apply the framework to two markets. For Taiwan, abnormal accruals and industry-adjusted inventory change exhibit no detectable leakage, and the associated long-short Sharpe ratio is bounded within 0.13. For the United States, where filing dates are observed directly through the SEC EDGAR system, profitability characteristics appear strongly distorted (filing latency correlates approximately-0.30 with industry-adjusted return on assets), yet the distortion is almost entirely attributable to firm size: larger firms file earlier and are more profitable, and conditioning on size reduces the partial correlation to approximately +0.09. The bias is therefore small in both markets, and the portion that remains operates through an observable characteristic that a size-aware release rule corrects. A companion paper develops that release rule; the present paper quantifies the magnitude of the bias it addresses.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTMany financial signals are same-period cross-sectional estimates-industry-relative ratios, abnormal accruals, any characteristic defined as the residual of a peer-group regression. Because firms file on staggered dates, a signal computed shortly after a fiscal period closes is estimated on the early-filing subsample; when filing latency correlates with the regression disturbance, the estimate embeds forward-looking selection ("incomplete-cross-section leakage"), a channel that standard temporal train/test hygiene (purging, embargoing, as-of joins) does not address. Existing remedies are all-or-nothing: release at announcement (biased for susceptible signals) or withhold every signal until a statutory deadline (a blanket timeliness penalty). We present a release-control system that grades the required cross-sectional completeness per signal from a directly estimable susceptibility primitive-the partial correlation between filing latency and the complete-cross-section disturbance given the construction regressors. Benign signals release early; susceptible signals are withheld until the cross-section is complete enough to suppress selection bias, up to the deadline. On a known-truth simulation-with the susceptibility primitive fitted on prior completed periods and frozen before gating (no oracle)-the controller releases benign signals at 36-39% completeness (vs. a 35% floor) with no detectable systematic bias, and escalates strongly susceptible signals to the deadline endpoint, inheriting its zero bias (naive-release bias -0.39). The companion analysis (Wu and Wu, 2026b) shows on observed U.S. SEC filing timestamps that the primitive separates signals whose apparent distortion is benign observable composition from those with irreducible selection; here that separation drives the gate. The controller ships as a reference implementation and is designed to sit upstream of feature-store materialization.
