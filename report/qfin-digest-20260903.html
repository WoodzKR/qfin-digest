ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThe non-Gaussian CAPM states that beta and coskewness are the only two systematic risks: beta captures second-order risk, while coskewness captures third-order risk. However, the empirical performance of coskewness is known to be weak. Using a return-moment expansion, I decompose coskewness into a sum of correlations between stock and market moments. If investors care less about higher-order risks, these components should receive unequal prices. In Fama-MacBeth regressions of individual stocks excluding microcaps, only the lowest-order component, the variance-variance correlation, earns a significant premium; higher-order cross-moment correlations do not. Thus, third-order risk is priced, but in a more nuanced way. A strategy combining beta and the variance-variance correlation earns about 9% annually, unexplained by Fama-French factors.

In a conventional monthly momentum strategy selecting the top N = 30 equities, replacing rank N + 1 with rank N incurs two full execution fee legs (≈ 42.34 bps round-trip) to capture a momentum-score differential that is frequently negligible. ORIGINAL ABSTRACTMany cross-sectional factors, such as industry-relative ratios, abnormal accruals, and the residual of a same-period regression, require the full cross-section of peer firms. Because firms file on staggered dates, a characteristic constructed shortly after a fiscal period closes is estimated from only those firms that have already reported. We term the resulting concern incomplete-cross-section leakage and assess whether it biases backtested performance. The conventional diagnostic, namely the disagreement between the early-filing signal and the complete-cross-section signal, does not identify the bias, because that disagreement combines three components: sampling variation from the smaller subsample, a difference in the covariate composition of the early filers, and any dependence of filing speed on the component of the signal under study. Only the third constitutes leakage. We isolate it by holding the covariate composition fixed, either through a matched control group or, equivalently, through the partial correlation between filing latency and the regression residual conditional on the regressors. This partial correlation is the selection-bias term of a Heckman model; because it is considerably less noisy than the disagreement statistic, we adopt it as the primary measure and provide it as an ex-ante screen, validated on a controlled experiment with a known planted bias. We apply the framework to two markets. For Taiwan, abnormal accruals and industry-adjusted inventory change exhibit no detectable leakage, and the associated long-short Sharpe ratio is bounded within 0.13. For the United States, where filing dates are observed directly through the SEC EDGAR system, profitability characteristics appear strongly distorted (filing latency correlates approximately-0.30 with industry-adjusted return on assets), yet the distortion is almost entirely attributable to firm size: larger firms file earlier and are more profitable, and conditioning on size reduces the partial correlation to approximately +0.09. The bias is therefore small in both markets, and the portion that remains operates through an observable characteristic that a size-aware release rule corrects. A companion paper develops that release rule; the present paper quantifies the magnitude of the bias it addresses.

ORIGINAL ABSTRACTThis paper asks whether stock markets predict the economy or shape it. Markets called the 2009 and 2020 recoveries before data confirmed them, which looks like strong evidence for market efficiency. But the Dot-Com bubble and the pandemic-era gap between rising indices and struggling households show markets can price sentiment as easily as fundamentals. Through the wealth effect, corporate investment, and policy responses, market movements feed back into the outcomes they're forecasting, making prediction and causation hard to separate.
