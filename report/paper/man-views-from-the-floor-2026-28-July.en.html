The Yield Trap Hiding in Junior Bank Bonds

Man Group

Body source: Man Group article

Man Group argues investors underprice the risk in junior bank debt.

1. At a glance

Man Group argues that Additional Tier 1 (AT1) bank bonds now pay close to the least they ever have for a risk profile that has quietly grown longer, more rate-sensitive and more documentation-dependent. The spread on the ICE CoCo index sits at 206 basis points against a 385bp full-history median, so the authors conclude AT1s have shifted from a credit bet on bank health to a levered bet on interest rates.
  • Valuation. AT1 option-adjusted spread (OAS) of 206bp versus a 385bp median — roughly 179bp below the middle of its own history, and near the tightest since the index began in January 2014.
  • Duration creep. Index duration has risen to 3.7 years from 2.3 years in 2023. Nearly a quarter of 2026 bank AT1 issuance carries a ten-year non-call period rather than the traditional five.
  • Extension and negative convexity. Low reset spreads locked in during tight markets make non-call economically rational for issuers. An uncalled AT1 behaves like a perpetual with 15–20 years of duration, about four times what was priced at issue.
  • Documentation dispersion. Some prospectuses let the conversion trigger ratchet upward without bondholder consent; others require a supermajority to amend it. Neither shows up in index-level spread.

This report is based on the full published article, not an abstract. The piece is investment commentary, not an empirical study: it presents no model, no backtest and no formal statistical tests. Where I formalise the mechanics below, I mark it explicitly as my own construction.

2. The gap it targets

The standard market frame for AT1s is a credit frame. Investors ask whether the issuing bank is solvent, whether CET1 buffers are thick, and whether the coupon compensates for subordination. On that frame the answer today is comfortable — the authors concede banks "remain in good health."

The paper's move is to argue the credit frame has stopped being the binding one. Three structural changes have accumulated while attention sat elsewhere, on AI capex and Middle East risk:

  • Spread compression has removed most of the compensation for complexity.
  • Issuance structure has lengthened, mechanically raising rate sensitivity.
  • Legal terms have diverged across issuers without any pricing consequence at index level.

The claim is therefore about risk factor migration, not default probability. The dominant driver of AT1 returns has rotated from issuer credit toward rates and extension. Index-level spread statistics cannot detect this, because they average over documentation and reset structure. That is the gap: headline OAS is a summary statistic that has stopped summarising the actual risk.

3. Method

The article contains no formal model. Its argument is a four-part qualitative decomposition of AT1 risk, supported by index statistics. Below I restate each mechanism formally so the economics are explicit. The formulas are my formalisation, not the paper's.

3.1 The coupon reset and the call decision

An AT1 pays a fixed coupon to its first call date, then resets. The post-reset coupon is:

\[ c_{\text{reset}} = r_{\text{swap}}(t_c) + s_{\text{reset}} \]

Here \( c_{\text{reset}} \) is the new coupon, \( r_{\text{swap}}(t_c) \) is the reference swap rate observed at the call date \( t_c \), and \( s_{\text{reset}} \) is the fixed reset spread written into the prospectus at issue.

The issuer calls when refinancing is cheaper than extending. Ignoring reputational effects, the rational rule is:

\[ \text{call} \iff s_{\text{new}} < s_{\text{reset}} \]

where \( s_{\text{new}} \) is the spread the bank would pay to issue a fresh AT1 today. The paper's point is that many recent deals carry a low \( s_{\text{reset}} \), fixed when markets were tight. If spreads widen so that \( s_{\text{new}} > s_{\text{reset}} \), the issuer keeps the cheap old bond alive and the holder is stranded.

3.2 Effective duration under extension uncertainty

An AT1's effective duration is a probability-weighted blend of two very different bonds:

\[ D_{\text{eff}} = \pi \, D_{\text{call}} + (1-\pi) \, D_{\text{perp}} \]

\( \pi \) is the market-implied probability of a call at first date, \( D_{\text{call}} \) is duration to that call (roughly 3–5 years at issue), and \( D_{\text{perp}} \) is duration if the bond runs perpetual. The paper puts \( D_{\text{perp}} \) at 15–20 years, "four times what was first priced in."

The damaging property is that \( \pi \) is itself a decreasing function of spreads. Widening lowers \( \pi \), which raises \( D_{\text{eff}} \), which amplifies the price loss from the same widening. Formally:

\[ \frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial s^2} < 0 \]

\( P \) is the bond price and \( s \) the credit spread. This is the negative convexity the authors name. Downside is amplified; upside is capped, because above par the call becomes near-certain and the price pins to the call price.

3.3 Duration lengthening independent of the call

Separately from \( \pi \), the primary market has extended the non-call period itself. Moving from a five-year to a ten-year non-call raises \( D_{\text{call}} \) directly. The paper reports index duration rising from 2.3 to 3.7 years on this and related effects. That is a 61% increase in rate sensitivity on the same asset class label.

3.4 Trigger and the buffer to conversion

Principal write-down or equity conversion fires when the bank's CET1 ratio falls to a contractual trigger:

\[ B_t = \text{CET1}_t - T_t \]

\( B_t \) is the loss-absorption buffer, \( \text{CET1}_t \) the bank's common equity tier 1 ratio, and \( T_t \) the contractual trigger level. The paper's fourth risk is that \( T_t \) is not always constant. Some prospectuses permit \( T_t \) to ratchet upward without bondholder consent, shrinking \( B_t \) without any deterioration in \( \text{CET1}_t \). Other documents require a supermajority vote to move \( T_t \). Two bonds with identical spreads can therefore hold materially different optionality against the holder.

3.5 The evidence procedure

The authors' empirical step is simple. Take daily OAS from ICE BofA indices, compute each index's percentile against its own full history, and compare AT1 percentiles to those of senior and Tier 2 bank debt and broad corporate indices. Figure 1 shows everything is tight but AT1s are tightest relative to their own history.

The stated conclusion

AT1s have "quietly shifted from a bet on a bank's health to a bet on interest rates." With thin spreads and long duration, rate moves dominate issuer fortunes as a return driver. The authors do not call the asset class un-ownable — they call it "a market for the discerning, not the desperate."

6. Limitations

Flagged by the authors

  • Documentation differences do not appear at index or headline spread level, so index-based analysis is structurally blind to them.
  • The credit case is not the problem — banks are healthy. The argument is entirely about price and structure.
  • AT1s "can play a useful role in a portfolio." The piece is a valuation warning, not a sell recommendation.

What would bite on replication

  • The 2023 duration base is a poor comparator. The Credit Suisse AT1 write-down sat in March 2023. Spreads were wide, prices were low, and market-implied call probabilities collapsed toward pricing-to-perpetuity in some names. Yet wide spreads also compress duration mechanically through higher discounting. Part of the 2.3 → 3.7 move is spread tightening, not term extension. The paper does not decompose the two. (My inference.)
  • The 385bp median spans a biased sample. The CoCo index starts January 2014 and includes 2016 Deutsche Bank coupon fears, 2018 Italy, the March 2020 crash, the 2022 rate shock and the 2023 Credit Suisse event. Five stress episodes in twelve years pull the median up. A median from a regime with materially better bank capitalisation would sit lower. The tightness is real; the magnitude versus a fair-value anchor is arguable. (My inference.)
  • No spread-per-unit-of-duration measure. The core claim is that investors are underpaid. The natural test is the spread-to-duration ratio over time, or spread against an estimated fair value from CET1 buffer, MDA distance and reset spread. None is presented.
  • Extension is not uniformly bad. A bond with a high reset spread that goes uncalled hands the holder a rising coupon. The damage concentrates in low-reset bonds. The paper says this but reports no distribution of reset spreads, so the size of the exposed cohort is unquantified.
  • No issuer-behaviour modelling. European banks have strong reputational incentives to call, and regulators watch AT1 refinancing. Pure NPV logic overstates non-call probability. The paper treats non-call as economically rational without weighing the franchise cost.
  • Timing risk. "Tight spreads" is a valuation observation, not a timing signal. Spreads can stay in the bottom decile for years while carry accrues. Shorting or underweighting on percentile alone bleeds.
  • Documentation screening is expensive. The authors' own edge case — trigger ratchet clauses — requires prospectus-level reading across hundreds of instruments. That is a real operational cost most allocators cannot carry.

7. Systematic trading angle

The paper implies four separable signals. Treat them as a scoring stack over the AT1 universe rather than one index-level call.

Signal 1 — Extension risk score (the core alpha)

  • Data: Bond-level reset spread \( s_{\text{reset}} \) from prospectus or Bloomberg DES fields, plus the issuer's current new-issue AT1 spread \( s_{\text{new}} \) estimated from its own curve or a rating-and-country peer curve.
  • Signal: the call gap \( g_i = s_{\text{reset},i} - s_{\text{new},i} \). Negative \( g_i \) means the issuer is paid to extend. Rank cross-sectionally.
  • Position: underweight or short the most negative decile against index or against high-\( g \) bonds from the same issuer. This is a structure trade, not a credit view, so same-issuer pairs strip out idiosyncratic credit.
  • Cadence: weekly rebalance; recompute \( s_{\text{new}} \) daily. Tighten to daily monitoring inside a 6-month window before any call date.

Signal 2 — Convexity-adjusted carry

  • Data: price, yield-to-call, yield-to-perpetuity, duration-to-call, duration-to-perpetuity.
  • Signal: the spread earned per unit of downside duration, \( \dfrac{s_i}{(1-\pi_i) D_{\text{perp},i}} \), where \( \pi_i \) is backed out from the price gap between yield-to-call and yield-to-perp.
  • Use: filter out bonds where headline yield looks attractive only because the market has already stopped believing in the call.
  • Cadence: daily compute, monthly rebalance.

Signal 3 — Buffer to trigger, with document weighting

  • Data: quarterly CET1 ratios and MDA distance from Pillar 3 disclosures; trigger level \( T \) and the amendment clause from the prospectus.
  • Signal: \( B_i = \text{CET1}_i - T_i \), then apply a haircut to \( B_i \) for bonds whose documents allow \( T \) to ratchet without consent.
  • Implementation: this is the one genuinely manual step. Parse prospectuses once per issuer, store the clause type as a static flag, refresh on new issuance. An LLM extraction pass over prospectus PDFs makes this tractable at scale.
  • Cadence: CET1 quarterly on results; document flags on new issue only.

Signal 4 — Index-level valuation overlay

  • Data: ICE COCO/COCE/COCU daily OAS versus ELT2 and EBSL.
  • Signal: the AT1-over-Tier 2 spread ratio, in percentile terms against its own history. The paper's 206 versus 385 is the level version; the ratio version controls for the general credit cycle.
  • Use: a beta dial on the book, not an entry trigger. Cut gross exposure when the ratio sits in the bottom decile; do not go outright short on it.
  • Cadence: monthly.

Hedging and execution notes

  • Rising index duration to 3.7 years means an unhedged AT1 book now carries meaningful rate beta. Hedge with swaps or futures if the intent is a credit position.
  • Negative convexity means the book loses more in a selloff than linear duration implies. Stress on a spread-widening grid, not on a parallel rate shift.
  • Liquidity is the binding constraint. AT1 bid-offer widens sharply in stress, exactly when the extension signal pays. Size for exit, and prefer same-issuer relative value where both legs stay quotable.
  • Beware the crowding overlap. Long AT1 sits inside high yield funds and specialist CoCo vehicles. In a redemption wave, forced sellers hit the same bonds.

8. Related concepts

  • Extension risk and call optionality — perpetual non-call structures, yield-to-worst versus yield-to-perpetuity, price-to-call pinning.
  • Negative convexity — the same mechanic as MBS prepayment risk, in reverse; useful cross-asset intuition for modelling \( \pi \).
  • Bank capital stack — CET1, AT1, Tier 2, senior non-preferred, senior preferred; TLAC and MREL requirements.
  • MDA (Maximum Distributable Amount) — the regulatory buffer that governs coupon cancellation before any trigger is reached; often the real binding constraint on AT1 coupons.
  • Contingent convertibles (CoCos) — write-down versus equity-conversion structures, high-trigger versus low-trigger.
  • Credit Suisse AT1 write-down, March 2023 — the precedent for AT1 loss absorption ahead of equity; essential for any recovery assumption.
  • Reset spread and mid-swap reference — the mechanics that determine whether extension helps or hurts the holder.
  • Spread percentile and z-score valuation — regime-conditioning, and why full-history medians mislead across capital regimes.
  • Covenant and prospectus NLP — automated extraction of amendment clauses, trigger levels and consent thresholds from bond documentation.
